# Own the Index, Not Another DMS

The firm's requirement set — client, handler and case-type metadata on every item, 100 GB+ of e-mail, SharePoint and file-share content in many formats, stored embeddings, fast search for humans and AI agents, and ingestion with no LLM in the loop — describes a **permission-aware search index with a metadata model, not a document management system**, and no shrink-wrapped product delivers all of those properties at once. The product the user half-remembered, Libra, is now **"Libra by Wolters Kluwer"** (acquired for up to EUR 90m in November 2025): an AI workspace that *links* SharePoint/OneDrive folders and WK legal content, priced at EUR 900–2,400 per user per year, with no public API or MCP endpoint — a consumer of a store, not the store itself. The DMS incumbents (NetDocuments, iManage) and the horizontal suites (Microsoft 365 Copilot, Glean, Amazon Quick, Gemini Enterprise) all index internally and **never expose their embeddings**; the only turnkey open-source platform with first-party Exchange and SharePoint connectors (Onyx) gates permission mirroring behind a paid Enterprise Edition, drops legacy .doc/.xls/.msg files, and has no rules engine for client/matter tagging. We therefore recommend a **self-hosted "assembled build"**: keep Exchange and SharePoint as systems of record; build a deterministic ingestion pipeline from mature open-source parts (Microsoft Graph delta connectors, libpff for PST archives, Tika/Docling plus OCRmyPDF, a local multilingual embedding model served by TEI); hold metadata, ACLs, BM25 and vectors in **one PostgreSQL instance (pgvector + pg_search) sized for ~10 million chunks**; and expose a single permission-filtered search API to a web UI and to an MCP server. The effort is bounded — roughly **7–9 person-months to a usable MVP and 17–22 to production, plus ~0.5 FTE ongoing** — because there is no generation layer to build, and the infrastructure is one 128–256 GB-RAM server (≈$18–25k to buy in 2026's inflated DRAM market, or ≈€320–460/month rented). Choose Microsoft 365 Copilot plus SharePoint governance instead if the firm has fewer than ~15 lawyers and no engineering capacity; choose NetDocuments (Germany region) or iManage Cloud if the firm actually wants a new DMS with e-mail filing and accepts a months-long migration. The analysis assumes an EU firm (Poland, Germany or the UK); the first section states where a US firm would differ.

## The report assumes an EU firm; a US firm relaxes residency, not confidentiality

The user did not state a jurisdiction. The research notes, and therefore this report, assume a firm bound by GDPR (or UK GDPR) and by one of the Polish, German or English professional-secrecy regimes, which together make data residency, processor contracts and a Data Protection Impact Assessment central to the decision. That assumption drives three conclusions below: the preference for a firm-controlled index, the requirement for hard deletion of vectors, and the weight given to the German § 43e BRAO service-provider rules, which are the strictest in the set and therefore the binding constraint for a multi-office design ([IWW on the BRAK guide](https://www.iww.de/kp/berufsrecht/kuenstliche-intelligenz-leitfaden-der-brak-zum-einsatz-von-ki-in-der-beratungspraxis-f165486)).

A US firm would weigh things differently in four ways. First, there is no GDPR DPIA duty and no erasure right over the index, so the vector-deletion engineering becomes a retention-policy question rather than a legal one. Second, the EU-US Data Privacy Framework dispute — upheld by the General Court in September 2025 but now before the CJEU with no hearing expected before late 2026 — is irrelevant to a US firm, whereas for an EU firm it keeps US-hosted SaaS lawful today but exposed to "Schrems III" risk ([Baker McKenzie](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/eu-us-data-privacy-framework-still-business-as-usual/); [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20251201-european-court-of-justice-to-review-challenge-to-eu-us-data-privacy-framework)). Third, the professional-ethics anchor shifts from CCBE/BRAK/KIRP/SRA to ABA Formal Opinion 512, which requires informed client consent before putting representation information into "self-learning" generative tools and rejects boilerplate waivers ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/american-bar-association-issues-formal-opinion-use-generative-ai-tools)). Fourth, the SaaS landscape tilts: Libra is a German/Polish product with no US footprint, while NetDocuments and iManage are US-centric and their US regions are the default. What does not change is confidentiality: US malpractice insurers now recommend "closed or controlled AI environments" ([Minnesota Lawyer](https://minnlawyer.com/2026/09/21/5-ways-malpractice-insurers-say-law-firms-can-reduce-ai-risk/)), and US panel-counsel guidelines such as The Hartford's require AI disclosure, prompt/input record-keeping and audit rights ([The Hartford](https://www.thehartford.com/claims-panel-counsel/case-management-guidelines/general-guidelines/use-of-artificial-intelligence)), so a US firm would arrive at the same architecture for ethical-wall and audit reasons even if residency fell away.

A second caveat applies throughout: the research sessions could search the web but could not open most vendor sites, regulator PDFs or pricing pages directly, so many figures come from search-result excerpts or third-party aggregators. Every such figure is labelled "third-party" or "via excerpt" below, and the cost tables rate each number's confidence.

## 100 GB is roughly two million documents and ten million chunks, and the hard problem is metadata, not vectors

Before comparing routes it helps to fix what "100 GB+" means in search terms, because it determines which engines fit on one machine. E-discovery heuristics put a typical gigabyte of mixed office and e-mail data at **50,000–100,000 pages, with 75,000 pages and ~18,750 documents the common planning figure**; image-heavy data runs ~15,000 pages/GB and plain text ~680,000 pages/GB ([CloudNine](https://cloudnine.com/ediscovery-best-practices-the-number-of-pages-in-each-gigabyte-can-vary-widely/)), and the UK judiciary's e-disclosure cost model uses the same 75,000 pages/GB ([judiciary.uk](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/JCO/Documents/Guidance/app19.pdf)). Those figures date from 2007–2012 and nothing newer was found, so they are a ballpark. Applying them, 100 GB is roughly **1.9–2.1 million documents and 3–10 million pages (central 7.5M)**, which at 450–700 tokens per page is 1.35–7 billion tokens and, at 512-token chunks with overlap, **3–16 million chunks (central ~10M)**; a 500 GB growth case lands near 50 million chunks. The e-mail side has its own shape: the Enron/EDRM corpus is ~40 GB for ~1.23 million messages, with attachments carrying ~91% of the bytes, so a 50 GB mailbox export means on the order of a million messages before de-duplication ([Nuix case study](https://de.slideshare.net/nuixdata/case-study-nuix-edrm-enron-data-set); [arXiv 1709.00362](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.00362.pdf)).

These numbers make the vector problem tractable on one server. Ten million 1024-dimensional float32 vectors are ~41 GB raw and ~61 GB as an in-memory HNSW index under Qdrant's 1.5× rule ([Qdrant capacity planning](https://qdrant.tech/documentation/cloud/capacity-sizing/)); Elastic's documented formula gives a similar ~42 GB working set for 10M × 1024-d at m=16 and shows int8, int4 and binary quantization cutting that to ~11 GB, ~6 GB and ~2 GB respectively ([Elastic kNN guidance](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/elastic/docs-content/main/deploy-manage/production-guidance/optimize-performance/approximate-knn-search.md)). Qdrant's published 400-million-vector run on a 64 GB box (float16 on disk, binary quantization pinned, 0.6–2.2 s per query) illustrates the far end of the disk-tiered trade-off ([Qdrant large-scale tutorial](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/qdrant/landing_page/master/qdrant-landing/content/documentation/tutorials-operations/large-scale-search.md)). The embedding pass is equally bounded: a bge-m3-class model under TEI embeds ~275 512-token chunks per second on an RTX 6000 Ada, so **10M chunks take ~10 GPU-hours and 50M ~2 GPU-days** on one card ([Runpod benchmark, via excerpt](https://www.runpod.io/blog/gpu-embedding-workloads-benchmark)). Even the API route is cheap for a one-off pass — $90 with OpenAI text-embedding-3-small to roughly $534–675 with voyage-law-2 or gemini-embedding at the central 4.5B tokens ([Voyage pricing, via excerpt](https://docs.voyageai.com/docs/pricing)) — although it sends client-confidential text off-premises, which most EU firms will exclude on policy grounds.

What is *not* bounded by hardware is the metadata requirement, and this is the crux of the whole decision. "Assign every item to a client, a handler and a case type" is a deterministic join against the firm's practice-management system, not a vector-search feature, and no product in any of the three routes does it for you. NetDocuments and iManage get it by making lawyers file into client/matter workspaces — a behaviour change plus a migration in which legacy e-mail is "complex and often incomplete" ([Serenit](https://serenitllc.com/blog/law-firm-document-management-it)). SharePoint gets it by making Client Name and Matter Number required columns so documents inherit them ([fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/sharepoint-law-firm-template/)), with the known failure that conventions drift unless tooling enforces them ([renamer.ai](https://renamer.ai/law-firm-document-management)). Onyx gets it only through a `.onyx_metadata.json` manifest you generate yourself, and only for its file-upload connector ([Onyx File connector doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/admins/connectors/official/file.mdx)). Products that *do* offer automatic assignment do it with an LLM at ingest — RAGFlow's auto-metadata "requires an available Extractor model" ([RAGFlow metadata docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/infiniflow/ragflow/main/docs/guides/dataset/metadata_management.md)), SharePoint Premium autofill meters $0.05 per page ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/vi-VN/microsoft-365/syntex/syntex-pay-as-you-go-services)), and M-Files' June 2026 AI agents that populate metadata are cloud-only ([M-Files press release](https://www.m-files.com/press-releases/m-files-ai-agents/)) — which the user has ruled out. The deterministic alternative is a rules cascade that every route needs and that the build route can own: a nightly PMS export as the source of client, matter, case-type and responsible-lawyer IDs; SharePoint columns and site-to-matter mappings; versioned regexes over file-share paths and filenames; and for e-mail the same header fields e-discovery uses for de-duplication (sender, sorted recipients, UTC sent time, subject, attachment names/hashes) ([ediscoveryplatform.com](https://sites.google.com/a/ediscoveryplatform.com/help/home/technical-notes-available/duplicates)) plus matter codes in subjects, a client-domain table, thread inheritance via In-Reply-To/References, and the mailbox owner as the handler candidate — with every assignment stamped with method, rule version and confidence, and the residue landing in a human review queue. No published source describes this cascade end to end; it is an inference from e-discovery practice and DMS conventions, but it is also the one piece of work that cannot be bought.

## SaaS: Libra is an assistant layer, the DMS incumbents sell governed stores, and Copilot is the cheapest path with structural limits

**Libra** resolves cleanly. Libra Technology GmbH (Berlin) built a "Legal AI Workspace" for law firms, ISO 27001-certified and hosted in the EEA, with research across Wolters Kluwer and Otto Schmidt databases and access to internal documents via SharePoint and OneDrive ([legal-tech.de](https://legal-tech.de/legal-tech-verzeichnis/eintrag/libra-ai/)). Wolters Kluwer announced the acquisition on 14 November 2025 for up to EUR 90m (EUR 30m upfront, the rest earn-out) and completed it on 19 November ([JUVE](https://www.juve.de/markt-und-management/wolters-kluwer-uebernimmt-legal-tech-anbieter-libra/); [Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/news/wolters-kluwer-completes-acquisition-of-libra-technology-gmbh)), and launched the platform on the Polish market in February 2026 combined with LEX content ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260203925761/pl)). Its documentation shows the architecture: connectors to SharePoint and OneDrive, Word and Outlook add-ins, and links to the WK practice systems Kleos and Legisway; a Libra "Database" can *link* a SharePoint folder and keep it in sync rather than copying it ([Libra docs FAQ](https://docs.libratech.ai/en/resources/faq)). Pricing per the official page (read via excerpt) is three yearly tiers at **EUR 900, 2,000 and 2,400 per user**, with SharePoint integration in Professional and shared "Databases" in Team ([libratech.ai/pricing](https://libratech.ai/pricing)); third-party reviews quote monthly figures of roughly EUR 200–233 per user that do not reconcile cleanly with the yearly list ([suitapp.de](https://suitapp.de/software/libraai/)), and WK legal content is licensed separately ([Libra docs FAQ](https://docs.libratech.ai/en/resources/faq)). No public API, MCP endpoint, index-size limit or metadata model was found. The verdict is unambiguous: Libra is a research-and-drafting layer that assumes the firm's documents already sit in SharePoint/OneDrive or Kleos in a client/matter structure; it does not ingest Exchange or file shares, and it cannot be the 100 GB store. A UK namesake, libra.law by Tabled Technologies, is an unrelated consumer-facing platform ([libra.law](https://libra.law/)).

**NetDocuments and iManage** are the two SaaS products actually architected as archival systems of record with e-mail filing, profile metadata and vendor-run agent access. NetDocuments runs separate regional stacks, including Germany (de.netdocuments.com, three German data centres) and the UK ([NetDocuments support](https://support.netdocuments.com/s/article/205217820)); launched Smart Answers (cited answers over the firm's own documents) in March 2026 ([NetDocuments](https://www.netdocuments.com/company-news/smart-answers/)) and a SALI-aligned Legal Context Graph in May 2026 ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/05/netdocuments-unveils-legal-context-graph-to-map-legal-knowledge-alongside-a-reimagined-platform.html)); and offers a hosted MCP server with regional endpoints (including Germany), OAuth, search/fetch tools, text-only results scoped to the authenticated user, and an explicit statement that content is not bulk-copied into outside AI stores ([MCP via ndConnect](https://support.netdocuments.com/s/article/MCP-via-ndConnect)). That MCP access is gated behind ndMAX Enterprise from 1 April 2026 ([NetDocuments blog](https://www.netdocuments.com/blog/netdocuments-expands-mcp-ecosystem-legal-ai-context/)). iManage's MCP Server reached GA on 14 May 2026, permission-bound and logged ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/14/3295124/0/en/imanage-mcp-server-is-now-available-to-connect-governed-knowledge-to-the-broader-ai-ecosystem.html)); Ask iManage became platform-wide at no extra cost in January 2026 ([Legal IT Insider](https://legaltechnology.com/2026/01/29/exclusive-imanage-opens-the-dms-to-natural-language-questions-with-new-ask-imanage-update/)); its cloud spans ten Azure regions including Switzerland, with a Swiss customer quoted that data "never leaves the country – not even for indexing or processing" ([iManage](https://imanage.com/resources/resource-center/news/imanage-addresses-data-sovereignty-needs-with-new-emea-data-centres/)). iManage Work still sells on-premises, but a 2024 comparison states all its AI capabilities are cloud-only ([iManage PDF](https://imanage.com/media/zc1lbish/imanagecloudvsonpremcomparison.pdf)), which removes the self-hosted-with-AI option. Neither vendor publishes prices: third-party ranges put NetDocuments at **USD 50–65 per user per month core with a 3–5 user minimum** ([LexWorkplace](https://lexworkplace.com/netdocuments-pricing/)) and one signed January 2026 order form shows ndMAX Enterprise at **USD 84.38 per user per month** for eight users ([CaseAgent](https://caseagent.com/netdocuments-pricing)); iManage at USD 50–75 per user per month with a ~10-user minimum and, in one consultant's words, a need for "an owner, someone whose job includes the DMS" ([LexWorkplace](https://lexworkplace.com/imanage-pricing/); [CaseAgent](https://caseagent.com/blog/netdocuments-vs-imanage)). The migration is the real cost: UK 30–40 user firms report 8–16 weeks contract-to-go-live with network-drive and legacy-system migration as the bottleneck ([comparethecloud](https://www.comparethecloud.net/articles/imanage-vs-netdocuments-vs-sharepoint-document-management-uk-law-firm)), others cite 3–12 months for a full DMS migration ([LexWorkplace](https://lexworkplace.com/implementing-a-document-management-system/)), and professional services run roughly USD 40–150 per user plus USD 2,500–20,000+ implementation ([Powell Software](https://powell-software.com/resources/blog/imanage-vs-netdocuments/)). Against the user's requirements the two DMSs score well on metadata, formats, e-mail filing, human UI and agent access, and poorly on three: embeddings are internal and not exportable; the ingestion of a legacy archive is a human filing project rather than a pipeline; and self-hosting with AI is unavailable. Exit is manageable — NetDocuments' web Export ships ZIPs with metadata ([NetDocuments support](https://support.netdocuments.com/s/article/ndWeb-Export-Tool-Available)) — but no documentation exists for exporting Context Graph relationships or Smart Answers indexes, so assume the AI enrichments are not portable. Epona365 is worth a mention as the SharePoint-native legal DMS that keeps everything in the firm's own Microsoft tenant ([Epona](https://epona.com/legal-software/epona365/)), avoiding a vendor-hosted store at the cost of inheriting SharePoint's limits below.

**The legal AI layers** — Harvey, Legora, DeepJudge, CoCounsel Legal, Lexis+ Protégé/Create+, Jylo — all sit on top of iManage, NetDocuments or SharePoint and leave the DMS as system of record. Harvey's Connector Library (GA 2026) offers MCP integrations to iManage and NetDocuments plus SharePoint, Outlook and DeepJudge ([Harvey](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/connector-library)); Legora's October 2026 integrations push states that "the original platform remains the system of record" ([Artificial Lawyer](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2026/10/15/legora-seeks-centrality-with-30-integrations-ecosystem/)). DeepJudge (Zurich) is the only one that is essentially a firm-wide knowledge *search* product with cloud, private-cloud or on-premises deployment ([legalai-review](https://legalai-review.com/product/deepjudge)) — and the one Gunderson Dettmer chose to replace its in-house "ChatGD" ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/03/deepjudge-introduces-ai-workflows-connecting-gen-ai-to-enterprise-search-of-law-firms-internal-data.html)) — but its customers are AmLaw and Magic Circle firms and pricing is sales-led. Seat prices across this tier are blog estimates in the USD 200–2,400 per seat per month range with 10–100 seat minimums ([Superkind](https://superkind.ai/blog/harvey-ai-pricing); [Spellbook](https://spellbook.com/learn/legora-pricing)); none was found to ingest raw Exchange or file-share archives. The 2026 convergence on MCP is the strategic point: a firm with a store that has its own MCP server gets agent access without buying any of these seats.

**Microsoft 365 Copilot** is the least-infrastructure horizontal path for a firm already on Exchange and SharePoint, and its limits are structural rather than priced. Graph/Copilot connectors give a tenant 50 million indexed items at no extra charge with up to 128 custom properties per connection ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/graph/connecting-external-content-api-limits)), which covers a legacy file share; Copilot surfaces only what a user can already view ([Kraft Kennedy](https://www.kraftkennedy.com/copilot-isnt-broken-its-being-literal-monitoring-and-troubleshooting-microsoft-copilot-in-legal/)); the Retrieval API lets agents query SharePoint and connector content, pay-as-you-go for unlicensed users ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/microsoft-365/copilot/extensibility/api/ai-services/retrieval/paygo-retrieval)); and Copilot is ~USD 30 per user per month on top of a base suite (third-party; [activepieces](https://www.activepieces.com/blog/microsoft-365-copilot-pricing-plans-and-costs-2026.md)). The structural problems map directly onto the user's requirements. Copilot Studio's official quotas state that "metadata filtering at the folder or knowledge source level isn't supported", that column-value queries are unsupported, that each agent is capped at 25 SharePoint sites and 1,000 files per source, and that sync runs every 4–6 hours ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/microsoft-copilot-studio/requirements-quotas)) — so an agent cannot ask "everything for client X handled by lawyer Y". SharePoint search itself maps metadata to a fixed set of refinable slots and uses only the first 5,000 term-store terms ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/search-limits)). The semantic index is Microsoft-internal and not exportable ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/DeployOffice/privacy/microsoft-365-copilot)). And Restricted SharePoint Search, which consultants call "the most critical control for law firm Copilot deployments" ([Copilot Consulting](https://www.copilotconsulting.com/insights/copilot-safety-blueprint-legal-privilege-confidentiality)), stops new enablements on 31 July 2026 and retires on 31 January 2027 in favour of per-site Restricted Content Discovery ([Directions on Microsoft](https://www.directionsonmicrosoft.com/in-brief/restricted-sharepoint-search-retiring-jan-2027/)) — a migration any Copilot-first firm must complete within the next three months. Azure AI Search ("Foundry IQ") is the natural Microsoft extension when those limits bite: hybrid indexes with your own metadata fields, vectors retrievable via the API, and SharePoint as a remote knowledge source ([Azure pricing page](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/pricing/details/search/)), at third-party-quoted ~USD 245 per S1 search unit per month and plausibly USD 1,000–6,000 per month at this data scale ([Turbo360](https://azure-cost-management-playbook.turbo360.com/docs/ai-search)) — but at that point the firm is building its own pipeline on rented components, which is the build route with a cloud bill.

Among the other horizontal suites, **Glean** honours Exchange Online and SharePoint ACLs at query time ([Glean docs](https://docs.glean.com/connectors/native/outlook/new-setup)) and offers single-tenant VPC deployment, but third-party pricing puts it at ~USD 45–75 per user per month with ~100-seat minimums (~USD 60k ACV) ([GoSearch](https://www.gosearch.ai/blog/glean-pricing-explained)), out of proportion for a 20–50 user firm. **Amazon Quick** (ex-Q Business; Q Business closed to new customers on 31 July 2026 ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazonq/latest/qbusiness-ug/what-is.md))) pools 25–50 GB of indexed source per user at ~USD 20–40 per user per month ([AWS Quick pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/quicksuite/pricing)), potentially the cheapest fully managed index for 300 GB, but with EU availability and an SMB connector unconfirmed. **Gemini Enterprise** charges ~USD 5/GB-month for ingested data stores and documents EU multi-region residency with connector caveats ([Google Cloud docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini/enterprise/docs/introduction-to-connectors-and-data-stores); [Google pricing](https://cloud.google.com/generative-ai-app-builder/pricing)). **M-Files**, the metadata-first DMS, is the closest conceptual match to the user's metadata requirement, but its 2026 packaging makes Aino, AI agents and the Copilot connector cloud-only, so an on-prem M-Files is not an AI-search strategy ([M-Files community blog](https://community.m-files.com/blogs-65091858/b/m-files-blog/posts/introducing-the-2026-m-files-platform-packages-and-pricing)). Every one of these suites shares the same two deal-breakers for this brief: embeddings stay inside the vendor's index, and metadata filtering is whatever the vendor exposes. Only the developer platforms — Azure AI Search, Elastic, Pinecone, Bedrock Knowledge Bases with your own vector store — let you read vectors back ([Pinecone API docs](https://docs.pinecone.io/reference/api/2026-04/data-plane/list.md)), and those are build components, not products.

## Open source: the engines are mature, the connectors are the bottleneck, and Onyx's permission sync costs money

The open-source landscape splits into three families, and the user's requirements land very differently on each.

**Search and vector engines are ready.** Every engine considered shipped in 2026 — Elasticsearch 9.5.6, OpenSearch 3.9.0, Solr 10.1.0, Qdrant 1.20.0, Weaviate 1.40.4, Milvus 3.0.5, pgvector 0.8.7, ParadeDB 0.26.5 — and native single-engine hybrid search (BM25 plus dense vectors fused by RRF or score normalization) now exists in OpenSearch, Solr, Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus, Typesense, Meilisearch and Vespa ([OpenSearch hybrid docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/opensearch-project/documentation-website/main/_vector-search/ai-search/hybrid-search/index.md); [Qdrant releases](https://github.com/qdrant/qdrant/releases)). The licensing traps are specific: Elasticsearch's RRF retriever is declared an Enterprise-licensed feature in source ([RRFRankPlugin.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/elastic/elasticsearch/main/x-pack/plugin/rank-rrf/src/main/java/org/elasticsearch/xpack/rank/rrf/RRFRankPlugin.java)), ParadeDB's community edition is AGPL-3.0 with a commercial Enterprise tier ([ParadeDB](https://github.com/paradedb/paradedb)), and Qdrant's default container ships with no authentication ([Qdrant README](https://github.com/qdrant/qdrant)). For a firm that wants a single operational domain, PostgreSQL does hybrid by composing two indexes in one SQL statement — pgvector for vectors (HNSW up to 2,000 dimensions, `halfvec` to 4,000, iterative scans since 0.8 for filtered recall) and either `tsvector` or pg_search for BM25, fused with an RRF CTE ([pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector); [ParadeDB hybrid guide](https://paradedb.com/blog/hybrid-search-in-postgresql-the-missing-manual)); pgvectorscale's StreamingDiskANN claims 28× lower p95 latency than Pinecone's s1 tier on 50M 768-d vectors, a vendor benchmark but a telling one about what Postgres can carry ([pgvectorscale](https://github.com/timescale/pgvectorscale)). Agent access at the engine level is also solved — official MCP servers exist for Qdrant and OpenSearch ([mcp-server-qdrant](https://github.com/qdrant/mcp-server-qdrant); [opensearch-mcp-server-py](https://github.com/opensearch-project/opensearch-mcp-server-py)), while Elastic deprecated its standalone server in favour of a built-in Agent Builder endpoint ([elastic/mcp-server-elasticsearch](https://github.com/elastic/mcp-server-elasticsearch)) and the reference Postgres server was archived ([modelcontextprotocol/servers](https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/servers)) — but none of them enforces per-user ACLs tied to the firm's identity, so they must sit behind an authorization layer rather than face agents directly.

**Open-source DMS products do not reach firm scale with firm-grade features.** Paperless-ngx is the only lightweight DMS whose 2026 feature list matches most of the brief — object-level permissions, typed custom fields, audit log, versions, workflows, mail ingestion with OAuth2, a Tantivy full-text engine and an opt-in LLM/RAG index that can use local Ollama models ([Paperless usage docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/dev/docs/usage.md); [v3.0.0 release](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/releases/tag/v3.0.0)) — and it is extremely active, with ten releases in seven weeks to v3.4.2 ([releases](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/releases)). But the largest documented deployment is **~360,000 documents on 8 vCPU/32 GB**, where the unfiltered list took 10–30 seconds before a 3.0.2 fix and a maintainer stated that installations this large are "not explicitly a goal" ([discussion #13161](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/discussions/13161)); its RAG index uses the pre-v1 sqlite-vec. A two-million-document firm corpus is beyond its design intent. Mayan EDMS is maintained (4.12.2, September 2026) but its GitHub presence is an abandoned mirror and its docs could not be reached, so its scale behaviour is unverified ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/mayan-edms/); [GitHub mirror](https://github.com/mayan-edms/mayan-edms)). Alfresco Community and Nuxeo are enterprise-grade but heavy — Alfresco's community compose alone runs the repository, Share, Postgres, Elasticsearch plus Kibana, a batch indexer, ActiveMQ and a transform service with ~8–9 GB of container memory limits before a realistic Elasticsearch heap ([community-compose.yaml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Alfresco/acs-deployment/master/docker-compose/community-compose.yaml)) — and Hyland increasingly steers their roadmaps commercially. Teedy has had no release since March 2023 ([tags](https://github.com/sismics/docs/tags)) and OpenKM archived its source and went binary-only from 7.0 ([GitHub](https://github.com/openkm/document-management-system)). The lesson is that the open-source DMS family does not solve the user's problem, and since the firm already has systems of record in Exchange and SharePoint, it does not need one.

**Turnkey RAG/enterprise-search platforms: Onyx is the only one with the connectors, and its edition boundary matters.** Onyx (ex-Danswer; 33k stars, MIT core) ships first-party, incrementally syncing connectors for SharePoint, OneDrive, Outlook/Exchange Online, Teams, Gmail, Google Drive, IMAP, S3-type stores and bulk file upload ([Onyx repo](https://github.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx)) — no other open-source platform surveyed (RAGFlow, Kotaemon, AnythingLLM, Open WebUI, Dify, Khoj, R2R, Morphik, Nextcloud Context Chat) has an Exchange or IMAP connector at all. Its SharePoint connector indexes libraries and site pages with delta sync and can mirror SharePoint and Entra group permissions every 30 minutes; its Outlook connector builds one document per thread (newest 100 of the latest 500 messages), appends attachment text, and can scope visibility to mailbox owners ([Onyx SharePoint doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/admins/connectors/official/microsoft-365/sharepoint.mdx); [Onyx Outlook doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/admins/connectors/official/microsoft-365/outlook.mdx)). The catch is in the same documents: **permission sync "requires Enterprise Edition when self-hosted"**, and the Community Edition's only visibility model is "public, or private to the connector's creator", with custom user groups and group-scoped access also behind the paid plan ([Onyx permissions doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/admins/permissions/understanding_permissions.mdx); [Onyx LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx/main/LICENSE)). For a law firm, that means a free Onyx can index every mailbox but every indexed mailbox becomes visible to everyone with connector access — a confidentiality failure across matters unless the firm pays. Three further gaps hit legal archives specifically: the connectors reject legacy .doc/.xls/.ppt ("convert first"), .msg and TIFF ([Onyx SharePoint doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/admins/connectors/official/microsoft-365/sharepoint.mdx)); Exchange on-premises is unsupported and the IMAP connector has no permission sync ([Onyx IMAP doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/admins/connectors/official/imap.mdx)); and metadata is tags-only via a manifest on file uploads, with no rules engine and no confirmation that the SharePoint/Outlook connectors expose path or mailbox as filterable fields. Onyx's own sizing guide is also sobering: OpenSearch needs roughly 1 GB RAM and 0.2 CPU per GB of indexed *text* above 50 GB, and the 10 GB-text worked example already requires a ≥9 CPU/35 GB single node ([Onyx resourcing doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/onyx-dot-app/documentation/main/deployment/getting_started/resourcing.mdx)) — manageable, because 100 GB of PSTs and PDFs typically yields 10–30 GB of text, but it argues for a 128–192 GB machine. What Onyx does deliver, uniquely, is permission-aware MCP out of the box: "agents get the same access controls as the user" and service-account keys inherit group permissions ([Onyx README](https://github.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx)). Onyx EE is therefore the right accelerator for a firm that wants self-hosting with a finished UI quickly, whose data is mostly Exchange Online plus SharePoint, and who will accept thread-level truncation, tags-only metadata and an unknown licence fee (no EE price list was found).

**The deterministic ingestion toolchain is complete and licence-clean, with two traps.** Apache Tika 4.x parses "over a thousand file types" and emits Markdown by default ([Tika](https://github.com/apache/tika)); Microsoft's MarkItDown converts Office, HTML and Outlook .msg with no model unless you opt in ([MarkItDown README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/markitdown/main/README.md)); Docling (MIT) reads PDF, Office, EML, MSG and images with layout and table models that are small vision models rather than LLMs, at **~1.2–1.5 pages/s on a 16-thread CPU and 4.2–7.9 pages/s on a consumer GPU without OCR** ([Docling README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/docling-project/docling/main/README.md); [Docling GPU docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/docling-project/docling/main/docs/usage/gpu.md)); OCRmyPDF (MPL) wraps Tesseract with 100+ languages and PaddleOCR/EasyOCR plugins ([OCRmyPDF](https://github.com/ocrmypdf/OCRmyPDF)); PaddleOCR 3.7 (Apache) covers 50 languages including the Latin-script set needed for Polish and German ([PaddleOCR](https://github.com/PaddlePaddle/PaddleOCR)); extract-msg and libpff/readpst explode .msg and PST archives ([extract-msg](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TeamMsgExtractor/msg-extractor/master/README.rst); [libpff](https://github.com/libyal/libpff)). The traps are PyMuPDF's AGPL licence (commercial licence from Artifex otherwise) ([PyMuPDF](https://github.com/pymupdf/PyMuPDF)) and Surya's model weights, which are free only below USD 5M revenue ([Surya](https://github.com/datalab-to/surya)). For the sync layer, Microsoft Graph's `driveItem` and `message` delta endpoints provide the incremental change feeds for SharePoint/OneDrive and mailboxes, including permission-change tracking for SharePoint ([Graph driveItem delta](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoftgraph/microsoft-graph-docs-contrib/main/api-reference/v1.0/api/driveitem-delta.md); [Graph message delta](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoftgraph/microsoft-graph-docs-contrib/main/api-reference/v1.0/api/message-delta.md)), and rclone's OneDrive backend handles bulk SharePoint pulls with delta listing and documented throttling caveats ([rclone docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rclone/rclone/master/docs/content/onedrive.md)). For embeddings, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Apache-2.0, 1024 dims, 32K context, 100+ languages, MTEB Multilingual 64.33 versus BGE-M3's 59.56) is the best CPU-feasible general multilingual model ([Qwen3-Embedding README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-Embedding/main/README.md)); but on the Polish-specific PIRB benchmark a 2026 preprint ranks PolDense-1B (64.11) and BGE-Multilingual-Gemma2 (63.26) above Qwen3-Embedding-8B (60.93) ([arXiv 2609.12913](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12913)), so a Polish-heavy firm should benchmark a Polish-tuned model on its own sample before committing, because every platform surveyed requires a full re-embed when the model changes.

## Build: a Postgres-centred stack fits on one server and needs 7–9 person-months to a usable MVP

The build route is smaller than the generic "enterprise RAG" estimates suggest, for one reason: the user has explicitly excluded the generation layer. Vendor and consultancy figures for a production-grade internal AI knowledge assistant cluster at **6–18 months and ~12–45 person-months (USD 250k–900k) with 0.3–1.0 senior engineers of ongoing maintenance** ([Kapa.ai](https://www.kapa.ai/library/should-you-build-or-buy-an-ai-knowledge-assistant-in-2026)), and those sources are written by vendors who want you to buy. Strip out prompt engineering, answer synthesis, hallucination controls and chat UX, and what remains is a data-engineering project whose components are all off the shelf. The research note's work breakdown — connectors (Graph delta for Exchange and SharePoint, libpff for PSTs, an SMB crawler), extraction and OCR, de-duplication and threading, the deterministic metadata engine, chunking and embedding, hybrid indexing, ACL/wall enforcement with audit, a web UI, an MCP server with OAuth 2.1, operations, and an evaluation harness — totals **≈7–9 person-months to an MVP (3–4 calendar months with two or three people) and ≈17–22 person-months to production (8–12 months)**, with ~0.5 FTE ongoing for connector breakage, parser edge cases, rule maintenance, spec upgrades and a re-embedding cycle every 12–24 months. These are estimates triangulated from the cited ranges, not measurements, and the schedule risks practitioners report are consistent: preprocessing and parser edge cases, document staleness and incremental re-indexing ("the RAG system had no concept of time, and every chunk was equally valid" — [HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/i-built-a-rag-system-for-our-analytics-team-it-worked-great-until-we-added-real-data)), chunking and relevance tuning, permission sync, PST corruption, and MCP churn.

The reference architecture is deliberately boring. Originals stay where they are (Exchange, SharePoint, the file share) and a copy of each extracted text lands on local NVMe or MinIO. **PostgreSQL 17 is the system of record for the index**: tables for persons, clients, matters (with case type, responsible lawyer, team and wall group), documents (SHA-256, source system and ID, parser version), many-to-many document assignments stamped with method/confidence/rule version, e-mails and attachments as family records, chunks with their embedding-model version, ACL/wall rows and an audit log. The same database holds the BM25 index (pg_search or `tsvector`) and the vector index (pgvector `halfvec` HNSW or pgvectorscale), so one SQL statement applies the identity-derived client/matter/wall filter to both legs, fuses with RRF and enforces row-level security — the smallest operational footprint and the easiest to audit. A single FastAPI (or Go) search service wraps it and is consumed by a web UI and by a purpose-built MCP server. Only if the corpus grows past ~20–50 million chunks or agent traffic becomes heavy does the design split into Postgres (metadata/ACL) plus Qdrant (vectors, payload filters, quantization tiers) plus OpenSearch (BM25 with document-level security, whose "adaptive" DLS mode runs simple term filters at Lucene level — [OpenSearch DLS docs](https://docs.opensearch.org/3.5/security/access-control/document-level-security/)) — still one physical server at 256 GB RAM, with the caveat that a second source of truth for ACL metadata must then be synchronized.

The access-control pattern is the one non-negotiable design decision. The index must carry matter IDs and wall metadata on every chunk, and the wall must be applied as a mandatory pre-retrieval filter at the database layer — "executing access filtering before vector similarity search" is described as the single most important design decision in a law-firm RAG system, because post-filtering lets restricted documents push permitted ones out of the top-k ([n8nlab](https://n8nlab.io/blog/rag-system-law-firms)). The leak to design against is subtler than returning a forbidden document: Kraft Kennedy warns that products securing only at document or folder level still expose the matter's existence in a lookup table ([Kraft Kennedy](https://www.kraftkennedy.com/how-solid-is-your-dms-ethical-wall/)), so facets, counts, autocomplete and "related matters" must apply the same filter and walled items must be indistinguishable from non-existent ones. Wall sources (Intapp Walls, iManage Security Policy Manager, or PMS team membership) are imported on a schedule with effective-from timestamps so an audit can reconstruct who could see what when; Nelson Mullins' use of Intapp Walls to override SharePoint permissions under Copilot is the commercial precedent for exactly this separation ([Intapp case study](https://www.intapp.com/case-study/nelson-mullins-copilot-ai-permissions/)).

Agent access follows the current MCP revision, 2026-07-28: stateless Streamable HTTP without an initialize handshake or session ID, mandatory `Mcp-Method`/`Mcp-Name` headers, JSON Schema 2020-12 tool schemas, an extensions framework, and OAuth 2.1 resource-server authorization with RFC 9728 protected-resource metadata, RFC 8707 audience binding and RFC 9207 issuer validation ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28-release-candidate/)). The firm should write a thin server exposing `search_documents` (with optional client/matter/case-type/handler/date filters that can only *narrow* the server-applied ACL filter), `get_document`, `list_matters` and `get_thread`, run it behind Entra ID or Keycloak, pre-register the Claude, ChatGPT and Copilot clients rather than allowing dynamic registration, and log principal, acting agent, tool arguments, applied filter and returned IDs on every call. It should **not** expose Postgres, Qdrant or OpenSearch MCP servers to agents, because they bypass the wall. The spec has changed four times in sixteen months, so budget one to two engineer-weeks per revision.

Hardware is the one place where 2026 is unkind. Server DRAM roughly tripled to quintupled year on year — a 64 GB DDR5 RDIMM was ~USD 2,064 street in August 2026 against a USD 450 contract price in Q4 2025 ([Capital & Compute](https://capitalandcompute.net/memory-prices/server/)) — and RTX 5090 listings sit at two to three times the USD 1,999 MSRP ([GPU Poet](https://gpupoet.com/gpu/learn/price/november-2026/nvidia-geforce-rtx-5090)). A sensible on-prem node (16–32 cores, 256 GB ECC, 2× 3.84 TB NVMe mirrored plus a 7.68 TB drive for originals and backups, one 24–32 GB GPU) therefore costs roughly **USD 18–25k, plus USD 6–10k for a CPU-only standby/backup node** — derived from the cited component prices, roughly double a 2024 build. Renting is comparatively attractive even after Hetzner's up-to-37% April 2026 increase: a GEX44 GPU server is ≈EUR 212/month ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/hetzner-to-raise-prices-by-up-to-37-percent-from-april-1)) and an OVH Advance-1 CPU node from ≈USD 124–134/month ([OVHcloud](https://us.ovhcloud.com/bare-metal/advance/)), so a GPU primary plus a CPU replica runs **≈EUR 320–460/month**. Because the originals already live in Exchange and SharePoint and the index is a derived artefact, the right resilience posture for a small firm is "rebuildable within N days" with nightly Postgres backups and snapshots to the second node, not three-node high availability, which would triple hardware and operations for an internal search tool. The GPU's real cost is idle time, so it should double as the query-time embedding and reranking server.

## Compliance pushes toward a firm-controlled index: DPIA, hard-delete, § 43e BRAO, CCBE and the SRA

The compliance analysis does not forbid any route, but it changes their relative cost, and it consistently favours the firm holding the index. A RAG index over client correspondence is a new processing operation — new copies, new derived data, new access paths and an AI component — and meets several Art. 35 criteria at once (sensitive data, large scale, innovative technology), so a DPIA should be treated as mandatory in every EU jurisdiction and under UK GDPR. The German data-protection conference published a dedicated guidance paper on RAG systems in November 2025 ([DSK OH RAG](https://www.datenschutz-berlin.de/fileadmin/user_upload/pdf/publikationen/DSK/orientierungshilfen/2025-DSK-OH_RAG.pdf)), building on its 2024 AI checklist ([DSK 2024](https://www.datenschutz-bayern.de/dsbk-ent/DSK_OH_KI_und_Datenschutz.pdf)); its specific recommendations could not be read in this research and should be checked before the DPIA is drafted. Legitimate interest under Art. 6(1)(f) with Art. 9(2)(f) for special-category content tied to legal claims is the realistic basis for indexing matter files, following the framing Mayer Brown uses for mailbox searches ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/internal-investigations-part-v-data-processing)) — which argues for scoping the crawler to matter-related repositories rather than every mailbox, and in Germany for consulting the works council first.

The erasure point has direct architectural consequences. EDPB Opinion 28/2024 set a high bar for calling anything AI-related anonymous ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/int/legal-updates/edpb-opinion-28-2024-key-takeaways-on-processing-personal-data-in-the-context-of-ai-models)), and while the Hamburg DPA's view that model weights do not "store" personal data concerns LLM weights, it cannot be stretched to a vector store whose entries are literal text chunks with metadata. Practitioner sources converge that such vectors are personal data, that query-time filtering does not satisfy erasure because data remains on disk, that many vector indexes only tombstone deletions until compaction, and that a complete erasure must cascade from the source record to vectors, payloads, caches and summaries ([tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/20/gdpr-llm-memory-erasure-vector-database); [mixpeek](https://mixpeek.com/guides/deleting-data-from-a-vector-index)). In the build route this is an embedding-ID-to-source mapping plus a hard-delete job with a documented compaction window; in a SaaS suite it is a question to ask the vendor, who may not have an answer. Legal hold cuts the other way — the index must never be the only copy, and Microsoft's own documentation shows that Copilot prompts and responses are themselves discoverable records held in a hidden mailbox folder subject to Purview holds ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/bs-latn-ba/purview/retention-policies-copilot)), which is a reminder that agent query logs in any route need deliberate retention rules.

Professional-secrecy rules are permissive in principle and demanding in detail. The CCBE's October 2025 guide lists "setting up AI systems to run locally or within a secured environment controlled by the law firm" as a technical safeguard and notes that most tools are cloud-based ([CCBE guide](https://www.ccbe.eu/fileadmin/speciality_distribution/public/documents/IT_LAW/ITL_Guides_recommendations/EN_ITL_20251002_CCBE-guide-on-the-use-of-the-use-of-generative-AI-for-lawyers.pdf)); its March 2026 technical companion describes an on-premise tier and "a dedicated machine to use with local AI models" as the budget option ([CCBE technical guide](https://www.ccbe.eu/fileadmin/speciality_distribution/public/documents/IT_LAW/ITL_Guides_recommendations/EN_ITL_20260327_CCBE-technical-guide-on-the-use-of-AI-tools-and-models-by-lawyers.pdf)). Germany is the binding constraint: BRAK treats every AI provider as a § 43e BRAO service provider needing a text-form contract with statutory minimum content, requires "comparable" secrecy protection for non-EU providers, and regards whether US providers meet that test as "not conclusively settled" ([BRAK Leitfaden](https://www.brak.de/fileadmin/service/publikationen/Handlungshinweise/BRAK_Leitfaden_mit_Hinweisen_zum_KI-Einsatz_Stand_12_2024.pdf); [IWW](https://www.iww.de/kp/berufsrecht/kuenstliche-intelligenz-leitfaden-der-brak-zum-einsatz-von-ki-in-der-beratungspraxis-f165486)), while the DAV concludes cloud/AI providers are "in principle permissible" under those conditions ([DAV SN 32/25](https://anwaltverein.de/newsroom/sn-32-25-einsatz-von-ki-in-der-anwaltschaft)) and German commentary treats locally run open-weight models as "part of the solution" ([beck-aktuell](https://www.beck-aktuell.de/rechtsbranche/legal-tech-digitalisierung/ki-kanzlei-open-weight-modelle-berufsrecht-2025-08-19)). Poland moved in 2025–2026: KIRP's recommendations for radcowie prawni (in force 28 May 2025) call for provider-contract verification, encryption, access control and "local processing of sensitive information" ([KIRP PDF](https://kirp.pl/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/REKOMENDACJE-DOTYCZACE-KORZYSTANIA-PRZEZ-RADCOW-PRAWNYCH-Z-NARZEDZI-OPARTYCH-NA-SZTUCZNEJ-INTELIGENCJI.pdf); [prawo.pl](https://www.prawo.pl/prawnicy-sady/rekomendacje-korzystania-z-ai-dla-radcow-prawnych,532905.html)), and the NRA added § 23e to the adwokaci ethics code in June 2026, requiring technological tools to be "exclusively supportive" ([NRA](https://www.adwokatura.pl/z-zycia-nra/zmiany-w-zbiorze-zasad-etyki-adwokackiej-naczelna-rada-adwokacka-przyjela-nowe-regulacje-dotyczace-korzystania-ze-sztucznej-inteligencji-w-wykonywaniu-zawodu-adwokata/)). The SRA's August 2026 warning notice — the UK's first — requires client data to remain "within a secure environment" and not be used to train models without explicit authorisation, and reports 42 misuse reports in a year ([SRA](https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/); [Law Gazette](https://www.lawgazette.co.uk/news/sra-looking-into-dozens-of-reports-of-ai-misuse/5127695.article)). The practical reading: a firm-controlled store with local embeddings satisfies every regime without a provider chain to paper; each SaaS link (vector DB, embedding API, connector vendor) adds a § 43e-style contract and, for US parents, a comparability memo. The EU AI Act adds little for a search tool — it is not Annex III high-risk, and the Digital Omnibus deferred those obligations to December 2027 anyway ([Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/digital-ai-omnibus-delays-key-deadlines-introduces-new-rules/)) — but Article 50 transparency duties applied from 2 August 2026 regardless of risk class, so the UI should label AI involvement and the firm needs an AI-literacy programme ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/)).

The documented failure mode in practice is not hallucination but over-retrieval. Copilot surfaces anything a user technically has access to, and a consultancy describes a firm whose permissions were set at practice-group rather than matter level, so Copilot surfaced privileged documents from screened matters ([Copilot Consulting](https://www.copilotconsulting.com/insights/copilot-safety-blueprint-legal-privilege-confidentiality)). The firms that report success — Clifford Chance's policy-before-rollout approach with lawyer validation of every AI output ([Legal IT Insider](https://legaltechnology.com/clifford-chances-early-generative-ai-and-copilot-journey-the-inside-view/)), Wedlake Bell's two sponsors and 27 champions ([Legal IT Insider](https://legaltechnology.com/knowledge-exchange-wedlake-bell-shares-copilot-adoption-strategy-and-experiences/)), DLA Piper's data-governance groundwork before wider rollout — share the sequence of permissions remediation first, tool second. A German practitioner's anecdote that "naive chunking just destroys context" and that hybrid search beat pure vector search ([Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1sl8a9t/snapshots/2026-04-14T20%3A07%3A31.723044Z)) matches the engineering sources. All of this evidence is vendor- or firm-self-reported; no independent, quantified outcome study of a law-firm internal search deployment was found, and no public post-mortem of a cross-matter leak either, which is itself a reason to build the audit log before anything else.

**Primary recommendation: Route C, the assembled self-hosted build, with Route A's Onyx as the accelerator only if the firm will pay for Enterprise Edition and accept its metadata and format gaps.** The reasoning is that the user's five hard requirements are jointly satisfied by only one route. Stored, portable embeddings and zero-LLM ingestion rule out every SaaS suite and both DMSs, whose indexes are internal and whose enrichment is vendor-side. A rich, deterministic client/handler/case-type model is work no product does for you, and the build route is the only one where that work produces an asset the firm owns rather than tags in somebody else's schema. Self-hosting is stated as a preference rather than the top criterion, but it coincides with the compliance posture that the CCBE, BRAK, KIRP and insurers all name as the safe default, and it removes the § 43e provider chain entirely. The build is not the open-ended "enterprise RAG" of vendor warnings because there is no generation layer: it is connectors, parsers, a metadata engine, one database and two thin front-ends, and at 100 GB it fits on one server with headroom to 500 GB. The firm's existing systems of record stay untouched, which also means the exit from this route is trivial — the index is rebuildable and the metadata lives in Postgres.

**Choose Route B2 (Copilot + SharePoint governance) instead** if the firm has fewer than roughly fifteen lawyers and no one who can own a Postgres service, or if it is already committed to a Copilot rollout. It costs ~USD 30 per user per month, needs no new infrastructure, and the governance work it requires (one site per matter, required Client/Matter columns, Restricted Content Discovery before January 2027, a Graph connector for the file share) is identical to Phase 0 of the plan below — so it is also the natural fallback if the build stalls after the MVP gate. Accept that agents will not be able to filter by matter metadata, that embeddings are not yours, and that selective SharePoint Premium autofill is the only automated tagging path.

**Choose Route B1 (NetDocuments Germany region or iManage Cloud) instead** if the firm's real goal is a new document management system — lawyers filing e-mail into matters day to day, version control, client portals — rather than an AI index over what already exists. Budget USD 50–75 per user per month plus the ndMAX Enterprise add-on for MCP, a 4–16 week (NetDocuments) or 3–12 month (iManage) migration with e-mail as the hardest and often incomplete part, and a named DMS owner; then layer Harvey, Legora, Libra or Copilot on top as consumers. If the firm is on Wolters Kluwer content (Otto Schmidt in Germany, LEX in Poland) or Kleos, Libra is the sensible *assistant* to buy once a SharePoint or Kleos structure exists — but it is never the store.

**Do not choose** an open-source DMS (Paperless-ngx, Mayan, Alfresco) as the primary store at this scale, e-discovery platforms as a permanent archive (per-GB hosting of 100 GB+ runs on the order of USD 1,100–7,500 per month before user fees, per third-party rates), Glean or Vectara at their seat minimums for a firm under ~100 users, or any API embedding route for confidential content unless the DPIA explicitly accepts it.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing from this research is that the firm is not choosing between three ways to store documents; it is choosing who owns the *index* over documents that already have homes. Once that is clear, the three routes stop being peers: the SaaS DMSs are a decision to change how lawyers file, the SaaS suites are a decision to accept the vendor's metadata model and keep the embeddings inside it, and the build is a decision to own a modest data-engineering asset whose hardest component — the deterministic client/handler/case-type cascade — is work every route demands and only this one lets the firm keep. The 2026 convergence of every serious store and layer on MCP reinforces that choice: a permission-filtered search tool behind OAuth is now the universal interface, so whatever the firm builds today will be consumed by Claude, Copilot, Libra or Legora tomorrow without a second migration.

Three forward-looking implications follow. The regulatory direction — DSK's RAG guidance, CCBE's on-premise tier, KIRP's "local processing", SRA's "secure environment", insurers' "closed environments" — is moving toward firm-controlled processing faster than vendors are moving toward exportable indexes, so the compliance cost of SaaS indexes will rise rather than fall. The hardware market is moving the other way: 2026 DRAM and GPU prices make renting a single-tenant EU server the rational default for the next year or two, and the architecture above is indifferent to whether the box is in the office or in a Falkenstein data centre. And the embedding-model question is the one decision the firm cannot defer or undo cheaply — every platform surveyed requires a full re-index when the model changes — so the Phase 0 benchmark on a Polish/German sample is worth more than any other week in the plan.
